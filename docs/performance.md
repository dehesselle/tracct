# Performance

This document records what the daemon costs and loses under load, as measured with the
benchmark matrix and the load test in `bench/` (see [bench/README.md](../bench/README.md)),
and how it holds up over a long uptime.

Measured 2026-10-27 on a Hetzner VM with 2 vCPUs and 4 GB RAM, Ubuntu 26.04, kernel 7.0,
on the current build. The load test ran the unit with its defaults (10 s write windows).

## Benchmark matrix

24 scenarios, 3 trials each: every scenario passes detection and bandwidth. Over the whole
run the daemon averaged 1.3 % CPU (p95 2.0 %) and 18.1 MB PSS. The matrix configures 1 s
write windows, so the interfaces and conntrack tables, read once per window, are read ten
times as often as with the defaults.

## Load test

CPU is the daemon's whole process, in percent of one core; PSS is proportional memory.
"Recorded" is the share of the generated bytes found in the database (see the load test's
notes on how it is counted).

| Phase | Load | CPU avg (p95) | PSS avg / peak | Rows/s | Recorded | Warnings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| steady | 300 idle TCP clients, 4 KB command lines | 3.2 % (5.7 %) | 29 / 29 MB | 70 | 100 % | none |
| churn | 30 000 new connections per second | 29 % (51 %) | 83 / 108 MB | 3 500 | 100 % | none |
| churn | 50 000 new connections per second | 44 % (83 %) | 130 / 158 MB | 5 833 | 100 % | none |
| exec | ~250 short-lived processes per second | 6.4 % (14 %) | 87 / 87 MB | 237 | 99.99 % | none |

The steady, 30 000/s churn and exec phases ran in this order in one daemon, so the exec
phase starts from the memory the churn left; the 50 000/s phase ran in a fresh one. Neither
the ring buffers nor the connection maps overflowed.

The reader resolves a process's executable once per drain, not once per drained
connection. Against the build before that change, run on the same VM, it cut the CPU of the
30 000/s churn from 71 % to 29 % and of the 50 000/s churn from 100 % to 44 %; the other
phases changed little. Reading each new process's command line and systemd unit at exec
time costs the exec phase about 3 points of CPU over not doing so, and is what keeps the
command line of a process whose last bytes are drained after it exited.

All of it ran with connection tracking read every window, besides a forwarded test flow
and a userspace TUN device; turning conntrack off made no measurable difference under
churn, with the kernel's table full at its 65 536 entries.

Memory under churn follows the distinct connections of a window, since the writer sums
events as they arrive; when it still held every event of a window, the 30 000/s phase
peaked at 441 MB and the 50 000/s one at 551 MB.

### Stalled writer

An exclusive lock held on the database for 90 s, under 30 000 connections per second:
every write attempt failed after the 5 s busy timeout and was retried, the retry buffer
stayed at 100 000 rows and the oldest rows were dropped with a log line, and the hand-off
queue dropped nothing. PSS peaked at 406 MB during the failed attempts and went back to
158 MB once the lock was gone and the retained rows were written.

## Long uptime

Two runs check that memory, the database and maintenance level off and that the daemon
recovers from faults. Retention takes fractions of a day, so turnover that takes months in
production takes minutes; a seeded database stands in for a month of history.

### Retention in minutes

38 minutes with 180 s of raw and 361 s of rollup retention, 1-minute periods and
maintenance every minute: about 12 raw turnovers and 36 passes. Besides the load generator,
ten processes with new names started every second, a transient systemd unit every 10 s and a
network namespace came and went every 30 s, with 8 bursts of churn and exec (60 s each, every
4 minutes). It ran before the maintenance changes below, which matter little on a small
database.

- PSS levelled off at 125–131 MB across the bursts.
- The database file stayed at 43–45 MB while 2.3 million rows were deleted; freed pages
  were reused.
- Maintenance took 0.4 s (median), 0.7 s at most.
- No lookup table grew between the middle and the last third of the run.
- Every burst was recorded at 99.98–100 %; no restarts, no warnings.

### Production retention on a large database

A database seeded with 30 days at 1 million connection rows per day (30 million raw rows,
as many rollup rows, 6.6 GB), 30 and 365 days of retention, hourly periods and maintenance
every 5 minutes, under the load generator for 2.5 hours.

Maintenance used to look for unreferenced lookup entries after every pass that deleted
rows, in one transaction: 120 s per pass (130 s at most) for about 3 700 deleted rows, with
nothing written meanwhile. Both churn bursts fell into such a pass and were recorded at 0 %,
3.3 million events dropped from the full queue; PSS stayed at 630–717 MB with the queue's
backlog, against 45 MB once the passes had nothing to delete. The search now runs once a day,
one lookup table per transaction: the longest takes 16 s, all of them 67 s. A 30 000/s churn
burst during it was recorded at 67 %, the same burst outside it at 100 %, and a pass without
it takes under a second.

The viewer's queries through the query service:

| Range | By executable | By domain | By remote address | Timeline |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 day | 1.1 s | 0.6 s | 0.6 s | 0.4 s |
| 7 days | 5.6 s | 3.5 s | 4.4 s | 2.7 s |
| 30 days | 18 s | 19 s | 19 s | 15 s |

Before SQLite's temp files went to the service's cache directory, the 30-day queries by
domain, by address and the timeline failed when its sort filled the 390 MB tmpfs of the
private `/var/tmp`. Interfaces and forwarded flows answered within a second at every range.

Faults:

- `kill -9` during maintenance: the unit restarted the daemon, `PRAGMA quick_check` was ok,
  and the next pass carried on.
- A restart during a burst and a restart of journald: the daemon came back each time.
- The wall clock 2 days ahead across one pass and then back: both passes were skipped and
  nothing was deleted early. Before that check, the same fault deleted 2.2 million raw rows
  early and left the rollup watermark 2 days ahead, which the next start moved back.
- Under `MemoryMax=1G` the unit's memory sat at the cap from the database's page cache, with
  17 MB of the daemon's own; nothing was killed.

## Indexes

Measured 2026-10-29 on the same kind of VM, on a seeded database of 5.8 GB: 30 days at 1
million connection rows per day, a year of hourly rollups (30 million rows), 1 500
executables, 200 units and 5 000 domains, each picked with a skewed distribution. The
queries ran through the query server's own functions, cold (page cache dropped) and warm.

Indexes on `(unit_id, time)` and `(domain_id, time)`, and `(unit_id, period)` and
`(domain_id, period)` on the rollups, in seconds, cold / warm:

| Drill-down | Without | With |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a unit with 1 % of the rows, 7 days | 2.0 / 0.7 | 1.3 / 0.2 |
| the same, 30 days | 8.7 / 3.0 | 5.3 / 0.6 |
| the same, 365 days | 16 / 10 | 10 / 1.1 |
| a unit with 0.04 % of the rows, 30 days | 8.9 / 2.8 | 2.4 / 0.04 |
| a unit with 30 % of the rows, 30 days | 14 / 8.4 | 11 / 7.3 |
| a domain with 0.6 % of the rows, 30 days | 9.3 / 3.1 | 5.8 / 0.3 |
| a unit's timeline, 30 days | 8.5 / 3.0 | 5.0 / 0.5 |
| by executable, no filter, 30 days | 27 / 21 | 27 / 21 |

Without them a filter other than the executable walks every row of the range. Cold, both
read the matching rows from all over the table, which the index does not change. The four
indexes add 1.8 GB (30 %, about 450 MB each), and the daily search for unreferenced lookup
entries takes 83 s instead of 116 s. Under the load test's churn at 50 000 connections per
second on that database, the daemon used 26.6 % CPU with them and 26.0 % without, and
recorded everything in both runs.

`ANALYZE` made things worse: with its statistics SQLite answered unfiltered queries through
the executable index instead of the time index, 11 s instead of 6 s over 7 days and 48 s
instead of 27 s over 30 days, and a namespace filter four times as slow.
